Menlo Park
The epicenter of venture capital and tech money on the Peninsula
$2.5M – $10M+ Established, leafy, prestigious VC partners, tech execs, old money
Key neighborhoods
Allied Arts / Downtown
$3M – $7M
Quiet, tree-lined, classic Menlo Park. Large lots, craftsman and ranch homes. Highly walkable to downtown. This is the neighborhood people picture when they say "Menlo Park."
Sand Hill Road, the address of more VC firms than anywhere on earth, is a 5-minute drive. The MLS pairs Allied Arts with Downtown, and you'll sometimes hear it called Stanford Park, after the Allied Arts Guild that names the area.
West Menlo Park
$3.5M – $8M+
The premium pocket west of El Camino. Borders Atherton and Las Lomitas school district. Bigger lots, more privacy, more old-money feel than central Menlo.
West Menlo is often described as "Atherton adjacent." Buyers who can't afford Atherton but want that feel end up here. Las Lomitas school district is a massive price driver.
University Heights
$1.2M – $5M+
A pocket within West Menlo, straddling Alameda de las Pulgas. Unincorporated San Mateo County, so it has a distinct country feel: no sidewalks, smaller lots, older ranches giving way to new two-story builds. Same top schools as West Menlo.
Because it's unincorporated county, not the City of Menlo Park, building and remodeling rules are generally less restrictive: a real draw for buyers who want to build. Las Lomitas / La Entrada schools drive the premium.
Sharon Heights / Stanford Hills
$2.5M – $5M
More suburban and spread out. Home to the Sharon Heights Golf Club. A quieter, more residential feel: less walkable but spacious. Popular with buyers who want room and quiet over urban proximity.
Sharon Heights Golf Club is a private club, membership by invitation. The MLS pairs this area with Stanford Hills, the adjacent hillside pocket: both feed Las Lomitas schools, the western exception to the east-west pattern.
The Willows
$2.5M – $4.5M
South of downtown, denser, more modest lot sizes. More accessible entry point into Menlo Park. Popular with younger buyers and families. Feels more relaxed than West Menlo.
The Willows is in the Menlo Park City School District (MPCSD), not Las Lomitas. That distinction matters and will come up from informed buyers. The MLS labels this area "Willows / O'Conner," after the O'Connor pocket within it.
Vintage Oaks
$2.5M – $4.5M
A small, established neighborhood dating to the 1940s on the east side, near downtown and the Willows. Smaller, walkable, more modest homes than West Menlo. A more accessible way into Menlo Park with a tight community feel.
Vintage Oaks is in MPCSD (Laurel/Encinal Elementary, Hillview Middle), not Las Lomitas: same east-of-El-Camino pattern as the Willows. Residents are known for a beloved neighborhood dog park.
Stanford Weekend Acres
$2M – $5M+
A tiny, semi-rural pocket of just seven looping streets tucked between Alpine Road, the Stanford Golf Course, and San Francisquito Creek, right against the Stanford Dish trails. Easy to miss. A mix of older humble homes and newer expanded builds.
On the MLS it usually shows under the alias "Alpine Road," not Stanford Weekend Acres, so listings here are easy to overlook: knowing that alias is a real local edge. Unincorporated county (looser building rules), feeding top Las Lomitas / La Entrada schools.
Linfield Oaks
$1.8M – $3.5M
A central, planned 1950s community of wide, winding streets wrapped around family-friendly Burgess Park. Midcentury ranch homes plus some two-story and modern rebuilds, with townhomes and low-rise apartments on the edges. Quiet, but minutes from both downtown Menlo Park and downtown Palo Alto.
On the MLS it often shows as "Middlefield to El Camino." Burgess Park (pools, courts, fields, skatepark, the library) is the neighborhood's anchor, and pedestrian bridges over San Francisquito Creek put you in downtown Palo Alto on foot. MPCSD schools (Encinal/Hillview).
Central Menlo
$4M – $8M+
The prestige core west of El Camino, between Downtown/Allied Arts and the Alameda. Large lots, classic ranch homes, heavy new construction and remodels. Alongside Allied Arts, this is where Menlo Park demand runs hottest: quiet grandeur, walkable-ish to downtown, and deep buyer competition.
When agents say "west Menlo demand," Central Menlo and Allied Arts are usually what they mean. It's in MPCSD (Oak Knoll/Hillview) despite being west of El Camino, one of the exceptions to the simple east-west district rule, so verify parcel by parcel near the Alameda.
Felton Gables
$3M – $5M
A tiny five-street pocket (Felton Dr, Encinal, Lennox, Arden, Tudor) between Encinal Ave and the Atherton border, built as custom homes in the 1930s–40s on the former estate of Senator Charles Felton. Larger lots around 10,000 sqft, narrow streets with oaks growing in the middle of some roads. A hidden gem unknown even to many Menlo Park residents.
Residents get private-gate access to Atherton's 26-acre Holbrook-Palmer Park for a small fee, an amenity most agents don't know. The honest caveat to share: Encinal Elementary drop-off/pick-up traffic can gridlock the only ways in and out.
Menlo Oaks
$2.5M – $5M
A rustic east-side pocket named for its namesake oaks: old, narrow streets without sidewalks, larger lots, and a country feel minutes from the Willows and downtown Palo Alto. More eclectic housing stock than the manicured west side.
Menlo Oaks is unincorporated San Mateo County despite the Menlo Park address: the same looser-building-rules pattern as University Heights and Stanford Weekend Acres, but on the east side. That's now three unincorporated pockets in one city: knowing all three is a genuine expert signal.
Flood Park Area (Suburban Park / Lorelei Manor / Flood Triangle)
$2M – $3.5M
Three friendly, active little neighborhoods between Middlefield and 101, clustered around county-run Flood Park. Post-war homes on modest lots, strong community feel, and among the most popular entry points into the Menlo Park market.
These neighborhoods famously fought to transfer from Ravenswood into MPCSD in the late 2000s and won: a piece of local history longtime owners bring up. School assignment here has real dollar consequences, so always verify current district by address.
Belle Haven (East of 101)
$1M – $1.7M
The only residential neighborhood east of 101, and the most affordable way into Menlo Park by a wide margin. Smaller lots, older homes mixed with new construction, and directly adjacent to Meta headquarters and the Dumbarton corridor. Meta's Willow Village master plan is reshaping the area's retail and infrastructure.
The key school nuance: Belle Haven is served by Ravenswood City SD for K-8, a major pricing factor, but still feeds Menlo-Atherton High like the rest of the city. Buyers weighing it are usually trading school ratings for price, appreciation upside, and proximity to east-side employers. Know the Willow Village timeline: it's the neighborhood's biggest story.
County Area / Fair Oaks Ave (North Fair Oaks)
$1.2M – $2M
An unincorporated San Mateo County area along the Redwood City border, parts of which carry Menlo Park addresses. More modest homes at some of the lowest price points in the Menlo Park MLS areas. A working-class history with active redevelopment and infill.
Despite Menlo Park addresses on some streets, this is North Fair Oaks: unincorporated county, generally Redwood City schools, not MPCSD. That address-versus-district mismatch trips up buyers constantly, and catching it before they do is exactly the kind of thing that earns trust.
One thing to always know
Menlo Park spans three school districts, not two: Las Lomitas ESD (West Menlo, University Heights, Stanford Weekend Acres, Sharon Heights/Stanford Hills), MPCSD (the central core: Central Menlo, Allied Arts, Downtown, Felton Gables, Linfield Oaks, the Willows, Vintage Oaks, Flood Park area), and Ravenswood City SD (Belle Haven, east of 101). Everyone funnels to Menlo-Atherton High. On top of that, three pockets are unincorporated county (University Heights, Stanford Weekend Acres, Menlo Oaks), and North Fair Oaks has Menlo Park addresses with Redwood City schools. District can swing value by $500K+ on the same house: verify by address, every time.
Palo Alto
Stanford's backyard and the highest price-per-sqft on the Peninsula
$2.8M – $12M+ Academic, walkable, intense Professors, founders, engineers, Stanford affiliates
Key neighborhoods
Old Palo Alto
$5M – $12M+
The most prestigious address in the city. Older, estate-scale homes on large lots. Quiet, private, and walkable to University Ave. The benchmark for Palo Alto pricing.
When someone says "I want Palo Alto" and has $8M+ to spend, this is almost always what they mean. University Ave restaurants are walkable: that matters to this buyer.
Midtown
$3M – $5.5M
Central, family-friendly, more approachable than Old PA. Good schools, decent lot sizes, strong resale. The "reliable" neighborhood: not flashy but consistently in demand.
Midtown is popular with Stanford faculty and long-tenured tech employees who want a stable, family-friendly base over status. Strong repeat-buyer profile.
Professorville
$4M – $9M
Historic district near downtown. Victorian and craftsman architecture. Named for Stanford professors who built here in the early 1900s. Walkable, charming, architecturally distinct.
Professorville is a historic district, so renovations require design review. Some buyers love the character; others hate the restrictions. Always mention this upfront.
South Palo Alto / Barron Park
$2.8M – $4.5M
More affordable entry into PAUSD. Quieter, more suburban feel. Barron Park is known for a relaxed, community-oriented vibe. Good for buyers priced out of Old PA or Midtown.
Barron Park has a herd of donkeys that live at a local stable. Locals love them and they're a real community touchstone. Knowing that detail signals you know the area.
Green Acres
$3M – $4.5M
A smaller, easily overlooked neighborhood on Palo Alto's southwestern edge, just south of Barron Park and bordering Los Altos. Built on old apricot orchards: mostly 1950s–60s ranch and bungalow homes with some new construction, and a quiet small-town feel. Juana Briones Park is the neighborhood's backyard, with Arastradero Preserve trails minutes away.
The draw is pedestrian access to a full top-ranked PAUSD school ladder: Juana Briones Elementary, Ellen Fletcher Middle, and Gunn High, all walkable. Don't confuse it with Green Gables, which is in north Palo Alto: a classic mix-up that instantly separates locals from outsiders.
Green Gables (Duveneck/St. Francis)
$3M – $6M+
The north Palo Alto umbrella area between Middlefield, Oregon Expressway, 101, and San Francisquito Creek. Contains several sub-districts: Duveneck/St. Francis (the largest and most prestigious, bordering Crescent Park), Triple El, Leland Manor, Embarcadero Oaks, and Garland. Traditionally the value path into north Palo Alto schools versus Old PA and Crescent Park.
MLS area #238, often labeled Duveneck/St. Francis on portals: know both names. Duveneck Elementary is a headline draw (ranked among the very best in California), and this side of town feeds Paly, not Gunn. The name comes from Eichler's 1950 Green Gables Addition (63 homes, National Register), his early work before the South PA tracts. Fulton Street is "Christmas Tree Lane" every December.
Crescent Park
$4M – $12M+
One of the most prestigious addresses in the city, rivaling Old Palo Alto. University Avenue runs through it, so its grand homes and expansive lots are literally the first impression of Palo Alto for visitors arriving from 101. Serene, tree-heavy, north of downtown near San Francisquito Creek.
Crescent Park is famously home to high-profile tech founders who chose it over Old PA for the privacy of its deeper lots. It pairs with Community Center on many portals ("Crescent Park/Community Center"), and median prices here sit in the top fraction of a percent nationally. Feeds Duveneck Elementary and Paly.
Community Center
$3.5M – $8M
Named for the civic cluster it wraps around: Rinconada Park and pool, the Lucie Stern Community Center, the Junior Museum & Zoo, the Art Center, the Children's Theatre, and the main library. Arguably the most amenity-rich residential pocket in the city, between downtown and Green Gables.
For families, this is the "everything within a stroller ride" pitch: park, pool, zoo, theater, and library in one loop. Walter Hays Elementary anchors the school story, feeding Paly. Portals often blend it with Crescent Park, so check comps under both labels.
Downtown Palo Alto
$1.5M – $5M
The most urban living in the city: condos, townhomes, and vintage homes within blocks of University Avenue's restaurants, offices, and the downtown Caltrain hub. On the MLS this covers the Downtown North and University South pockets on either side of University Ave.
This is where Palo Alto's condo market lives, which makes it the entry point for buyers who want the address without a $3M+ budget, and the downsizing destination for empty nesters leaving big Old PA and Crescent Park lots. Two distinct sub-markets: Downtown North (quieter, leafier) and University South (closer to Professorville, more prewar charm).
College Terrace
$2.2M – $4M
A 12-block-by-2-block pocket wedged between Stanford and the California Avenue district, with streets named after universities and colleges. Older than Palo Alto itself: founded 1887, part of old Mayfield, annexed in 1925. Eclectic mix of cottages, bungalows, and rentals with a strong academic flavor.
Stanford actively buys homes here for faculty, a source of local consternation but also price support: a genuinely insider dynamic to know. It had Palo Alto's first residential parking permit program (Stanford spillover). Walkable to Cal Ave, Caltrain, and the Research Park employers. The adjacent Evergreen Park and Southgate pockets offer similar charm and Cal Ave walkability.
Ventura
$1.9M – $3M
A small, eclectic neighborhood between El Camino and the Caltrain line, roughly Oregon Expressway to Page Mill. A mix of apartments, townhomes, and smaller single-family homes (many under 1,500 sqft) on smaller lots. Walkable to the California Avenue business district and its Caltrain station. The closest thing to an affordable entry into Palo Alto.
About three-quarters of Ventura residents are renters, highly unusual for Palo Alto. It's also widely seen as the Palo Alto neighborhood most likely to transform in the coming years given its stock of smaller older homes and redevelopment talk (locals know the former Fry's Electronics site conversation). Boulware Park is the anchor.
The Eichler Tracts (Greenmeadow, Fairmeadow, Charleston Meadows)
$3M – $5M
South Palo Alto is the Eichler capital of the world: Joseph Eichler built 2,700+ homes in Palo Alto, more than any other city. Greenmeadow (1954, ~270 homes) is on the National Register of Historic Places. Fairmeadow, called "The Circles" for its concentric street layout, borders Mitchell Park. Charleston Meadows has some of the earliest tracts (1951). Palo Verde and Greer Park have solid Eichler stock too.
Eichler buyers are a self-selecting tribe: they ask for tracts by name and expect you to know clerestory windows, post-and-beam, atriums, and glass rear walls. Know the trade-offs too (single-pane glass, radiant slab heating, flat roofs) and that Greenmeadow's historic status adds scrutiny to alterations, like Professorville.
Palo Alto Hills
$4M – $10M+
The city's estate country: large lots in the southwestern foothills around the Palo Alto Hills Golf & Country Club, bordering the Foothills Nature Preserve and Arastradero Preserve trails. Semi-rural, view-driven, and a completely different product from the flats: privacy and land instead of walkability.
This is Palo Alto's answer to Los Altos Hills, with PAUSD schools as the differentiator (Gunn feed). Buyers here cross-shop Los Altos Hills and Portola Valley, so know that comparison: PA Hills trades a bit of lot size for the PAUSD label. Winding roads and fire-zone insurance questions come up: be ready for both.
One thing to always know
PAUSD (Palo Alto Unified) is the main school district and commands a massive premium. But watch for the edges: some South Palo Alto streets feed into Los Altos School District instead, which buyers in the know will test you on. Also know which high school a neighborhood feeds: south/west neighborhoods like Green Acres and Barron Park go to Gunn High, while north-central neighborhoods go to Palo Alto High (Paly). Buyers often target one specifically. Verify district boundaries before you open your mouth about schools.
Atherton
The wealthiest zip code in America: no downtown, no commercial, just estates
$5M – $30M+ Ultra-private, estate, old money Billionaires, tech founders, hedge fund, inherited wealth
Key areas (Atherton has no official neighborhoods: it functions as one unified enclave)
West Atherton
$8M – $30M+
The most private and prestigious section. Very large lots, gated driveways, extreme seclusion. Trophy properties and generational estates. The highest price tier in the city.
Properties here rarely come to market publicly. Many trade off-market. If you're showing a buyer something in West Atherton, it's a serious transaction.
Central Atherton
$5M – $12M
Still exceptional but slightly more accessible than the west side. Mix of original estates and newer construction. Atherton's "entry point," which means it starts at $5M.
Buyers who say "I want Atherton" but have $5–6M are looking here. A good comp here can still clear $1,500/sqft. Knowing that number cold impresses clients.
Atherton / Menlo border
$4M – $8M
The eastern edge blending into Menlo Park. Same school district access (Las Lomitas), slightly lower price than deep Atherton. Popular with buyers who want the feel without the top-tier price.
Listings here sometimes market themselves as "Atherton-adjacent" or even just "Atherton" informally. Always verify the actual city boundary before presenting to a client.
Lindenwood
$5M – $15M
A gated community within Atherton. HOA governs the road maintenance. Exceptionally private. One of the most recognized addresses even within Atherton.
Lindenwood has private roads maintained by residents. Some buyers love the extra layer of privacy; others see HOA involvement as a negative. Know before you show.
One thing to always know
Atherton has no commercial zone: no cafes, no restaurants, no shops. It's entirely residential by design. The city's identity is built around privacy and exclusion. Buyers coming from urban environments sometimes don't realize this upfront. Mention it early so there are no surprises.
Redwood City
The most accessible and diverse entry point into Peninsula real estate
$1.4M – $4M+ Energetic, diverse, up-and-coming First-time Peninsula buyers, young families, investors
Key neighborhoods
Farm Hill Estates / Cordilleras Heights / Emerald Hills
$2M – $4M
Elevated hillside neighborhoods with views, larger lots, and a quieter suburban feel. More space for the money than flat Redwood City. Emerald Hills holds some of the largest and most expensive homes in the hills, up long winding roads with big lots. Emerald Lake, the Elks Lodge, and Emerald Hills Golf Course anchor the area.
The difference between Farm Hill Estates and Emerald Hills comes down to one word: annexation. Farm Hill Estates is incorporated Redwood City; Emerald Hills is unincorporated San Mateo County. Same hills, different rules and services. Buyers who discover the area often say it doesn't feel like what they expected from "Redwood City."
Mount Carmel
$1.6M – $2.8M
Central, walkable, close to downtown and Caltrain. A mix of older craftsman and post-war homes. Strong rental demand. Good entry-level for Peninsula buyers.
Mount Carmel sits inside the larger High School Acres MLS area, along with the Wesley/Edgewood Park pocket. Both are prized for large level lots, stately homes, and curb appeal. Downtown Redwood City has transformed in the past decade, and Mount Carmel buyers benefit from that without paying downtown premiums. North Star Academy (3-8) is here and is one of the district's most sought-after schools.
Redwood Shores
$1.4M – $2.5M
A planned waterfront community on the bay. Condos, townhomes, and single-family homes. More affordable, very popular with commuters to Oracle HQ (located there) and other bay-side tech campuses.
Redwood Shores has a lagoon system: some homes are waterfront and command a premium. Those listings sell fast. It's technically in Redwood City but feels like its own world.
Kentfield Commons / Roy Cloud area
$2.2M – $3.5M
Gated community feel, very family-oriented, near the Atherton border. Roy Cloud elementary is a draw. Quieter and more suburban than central Redwood City.
The Atherton adjacency here, similar to West Menlo, adds a psychological premium that shows up in prices. Buyers feel like they're in that world without paying Atherton prices.
High School Acres
$1.6M – $3M
One of Redwood City's oldest neighborhoods, bounded by Roosevelt Ave, El Camino, Alameda de las Pulgas, and the San Carlos border. Vintage homes with real character, and a wide mix of price points from expensive to relatively affordable. Contains the Mount Carmel and Wesley/Edgewood Park sub-neighborhoods.
Named for Sequoia High School, the first high school in San Mateo County: it opened downtown in 1895 in the building that is now the Fox Theater, and moved to Brewster Ave in 1924. Stafford Park is the community anchor, with summer concerts and family gatherings. That history is a genuine local-fluency detail.
Horgan Ranch
$1.7M – $3M
The Redwood City neighborhood closest to Atherton, running from El Camino west to Alameda de las Pulgas, between Woodside Road and Stockbridge Ave. Bigger-than-average lots, a heavily wooded look, and a noticeable absence of sidewalks that gives parts of it a rural feel. Includes the Redwood Oaks and Selby Park sub-neighborhoods.
This was once the epicenter of the world's chrysanthemum-growing industry, built by Japanese immigrant growers who founded the Chrysanthemum Growers Association in 1937. Their land was maintained by neighbors during WWII internment. The old five-acre subdivided lots are why lots here run larger than the Redwood City norm. Streets ending at Stockbridge back directly onto Atherton.
Central Park
$1.5M – $2.5M
Flat and walkable, running from El Camino west to Alameda de las Pulgas, and from Woodside Road north to Jefferson Ave. Mostly 1940s and 50s homes. Increasingly popular for its balance of affordability and desirability. Contains Palm Park on the eastern half and Woodside Plaza on the western portion.
Woodside Plaza is the name buyers actually use for the western half, so know both labels when pulling comps. This area generally feeds Kennedy Middle and Woodside High rather than Sequoia, a distinction worth verifying by address.
Clifford Heights
$2M – $4M
North of the Alameda and west of Edgewood Road, on the hillside side of town. One of Redwood City's most desirable and least-known areas, with fewer than a thousand residents. Also encompasses Eaton Meadows, Laurel Terrace, the Maxwell Tract, and Palomar Park.
Often referred to interchangeably as Palomar Park, and it's unincorporated San Mateo County: another county pocket, like Emerald Hills. Clifford Elementary (K-8) serves the area. It's so small and quiet that residents joke neighboring Emerald Hills feels crowded.
Lenolt (the ABC streets)
$1.2M – $1.9M
On the Redwood City/San Carlos border off Veterans Blvd and El Camino, roughly a mile from downtown and walkable to Caltrain. A mix of single-family homes, apartments, and some industrial, plus Kaiser and big-box retail. The MLS "Lenolt" area also folds in Staumbaugh Heller and Centennial, which includes downtown.
Called "the ABC streets" because they're named in alphabetical order, which makes them instantly identifiable on a map. This is one of the best first-time-buyer entries on the Peninsula: a single-family home here can cost what a townhome costs in neighboring San Carlos. Served by Hoover, Spinas, Linden, and Fleishman parks.
Dumbarton
$1M – $1.8M
The far southeast corner of Redwood City, bordering east Menlo Park. One of the largest MLS areas, encompassing multiple sub-neighborhoods including North Fair Oaks, Redwood Village, Eastern Addition, and Friendly Acres. The most diverse and most affordable part of the city, with an industrial spine running down Bay Road.
Friendly Acres, right off 101, has seen the sharpest change as tech money moves in. Homes are small, often well under 1,500 sqft single-story, with additions and remodels appearing constantly. Note the overlap with the North Fair Oaks area that also carries some Menlo Park addresses: verify city and district by parcel, never by address feel.
One thing to always know
Redwood City's MLS areas run in a numbered west-to-east logic worth memorizing: 330 Dumbarton, 331 Lenolt, 332 Horgan Ranch, 333 Central Park, 334 High School Acres, 335 Farm Hill Estates, 336 Cordilleras Heights/Emerald Hills, 337 Clifford Heights, plus Redwood Shores on the bay. Prices generally climb as you move west and uphill. High school is Sequoia Union HSD, but which campus varies: Sequoia High serves High School Acres and nearby, while Central Park and Horgan Ranch areas generally feed Woodside High. Elementary varies neighborhood by neighborhood (Roy Cloud, Clifford, John Gill, Henry Ford, North Star Academy, Hoover, Kennedy). Two unincorporated county pockets to know: Emerald Hills and Clifford Heights/Palomar Park.
San Carlos
"The City of Good Living": the Peninsula's most beloved family town
$1.5M – $3.5M Charming, walkable, tight-knit Young families, move-up buyers, community-oriented
Key neighborhoods
White Oaks
$2.2M – $3.5M
The most desirable neighborhood in San Carlos. Tree-lined streets, larger lots, pride-of-ownership homes. Short walk to Laurel Street. The address families work toward in San Carlos.
Homes in White Oaks often sell with multiple offers regardless of market conditions. If your buyer wants San Carlos and has the budget, start here: it holds value exceptionally well.
Brittan Acres
$1.8M – $2.8M
Flat, grid-pattern streets west of El Camino. Very family-friendly, close to Burton Park. One of the most kid-friendly pockets in the city, with a high density of young families.
Burton Park is the center of community life here: Saturday little league, picnics, community events. Mentioning it tells buyers you know what daily life actually looks like in this neighborhood.
Howard Park
$1.6M – $2.5M
East of El Camino, closer to the bay. More modest and affordable entry into San Carlos. Mix of older ranch homes and some newer builds. Good for buyers stretching into the market.
East of El Camino generally means lower prices anywhere on the Peninsula. That's a rule of thumb worth internalizing: it applies to San Carlos, Menlo Park, and Redwood City alike.
Central San Carlos / Laurel St area
$1.7M – $3M
Walking distance to Laurel Street's restaurants, shops, and coffee. The "downtown adjacent" pocket. Buyers who want urban walkability without going to Redwood City choose this.
Laurel Street is a legitimate dining destination, not just a strip mall. San Carlos residents take pride in it. Knowing a few restaurant names (like The Refuge for beer and sandwiches) signals real local knowledge.
One thing to always know
San Carlos Elementary SD covers K–8. High school goes to Carlmont High School via Sequoia Union HSD. Carlmont is physically located in Belmont (not San Carlos): knowing that geography prevents confusion when buyers look it up on a map and wonder why the high school address doesn't match the city.
Belmont
Underrated hillside city: Carlmont High's actual home and a quiet value play
$1.3M – $2.8M Quiet, hillside, residential Practical buyers, value seekers, families wanting space
Key neighborhoods
Belmont Hills
$1.8M – $2.8M+
The premium pocket. Elevated, with bay views and canyon views. Larger lots. More dramatic landscape than flat Peninsula neighborhoods. The place buyers end up when they discover Belmont.
Views from Belmont Hills on a clear day include the Bay, the Dumbarton Bridge, and on exceptional days the East Bay hills. Always check the weather before showing hillside homes here.
Old Belmont / Central
$1.3M – $2.2M
Flat to gently rolling. Older neighborhood stock, quiet streets. The most affordable and accessible part of Belmont. Good for buyers who want a single-family home without a steep driveway.
Many buyers discover Belmont after being outbid in San Carlos repeatedly. The pitch: same Carlmont High, same Sequoia Union HSD, $200–300K less. That's a compelling value case to make.
Belmont Village
$1.4M – $2M
Near Belmont's small downtown strip. Walkable to the Caltrain station, which is a draw for SF commuters. More urban density, smaller lots, but convenient location.
Caltrain access here is underrated for SF commuters who can't afford to live in the city. The commute is clean: 35–40 min to SF by express. Lean into this with that buyer profile.
Rancho Cielo / Carlmont area
$1.5M – $2.5M
Near Carlmont High School itself. Family-oriented, convenient to Caltrain and 101. Comfortable suburban pocket that benefits from the high school's proximity and reputation.
Buyers with high schoolers specifically seek walkability to Carlmont. Mentioning which streets are within walking distance is a detail that earns trust with that buyer type.
One thing to always know
Belmont's elementary and middle school falls under Belmont-Redwood Shores SD (K–8). This is the same district that covers Redwood Shores: buyers sometimes get confused when they see the district name and think Redwood Shores, not Belmont. High school is Carlmont via Sequoia Union HSD.
Los Altos
Village charm, orchard heritage, and estate country up the hill
$2.5M – $15M+ Quiet, leafy, understated wealth Tech execs, families trading up, privacy seekers
Key neighborhoods
North Los Altos
$3.5M – $9M+
The market leader and the first part of town developed. Contains Old Los Altos, which has an Old Palo Alto feel: mature tree canopy, estates on University Avenue (some over 100 years old), and true walkability to downtown. Most homes sit on 5,000–7,000 sqft lots off the big University Ave parcels.
Locals call downtown "the Village," and walk-to-Village is the single biggest premium driver in Los Altos. North Los Altos posted the city's highest average price and claimed both the highest and lowest sales of 2024, the low being an SB9 developer play.
Country Club
$2.5M – $9M
Perched along the foothills by the San Antonio Open Space Preserve, named for the member-owned Los Altos Golf & Country Club. Spacious lots, generally a quarter to a full acre, with views of the club's rolling hills. Eclectic mix of home styles and sizes.
Despite the Los Altos address, Country Club is unincorporated Santa Clara County: county building standards are more liberal, so you can build bigger and faster than in the city proper. Developers know this and actively target the area. Same pattern as University Heights in Menlo Park. Bonus history: the land was originally bought by Santa Clara University for a second campus before the 1906 earthquake killed the plan.
Highlands
$3M – $7.5M
The south-side area toward the Cupertino border. Larger, quieter, more suburban in feel, further from the Village. Recorded the city's lowest average price and longest days on market in 2024, which makes it the relative value play within Los Altos.
The critical detail: the Highlands feeds Cupertino schools (Cupertino Union / Fremont Union HSD, including Homestead High), not Los Altos schools. Homes feeding MVLA (Mountain View-Los Altos High) generally sell for more than Cupertino-feeding homes. Know which side of that line a listing is on before quoting schools.
South of El Monte
$2M – $7M
The area south of El Monte Avenue, spanning a wide price range from entry-level Los Altos to substantial rebuilds. Includes pockets like Loyola Corners, the small foothill-adjacent commercial node locals treat as their neighborhood hub. Quieter and more residential than the north side.
2024 sales here ranged from about $2.1M to $7M, one of the widest spreads in the city. That spread is your opportunity: buyers priced out of North Los Altos can still get Los Altos schools here, if you verify the district boundary street by street.
Los Altos Hills
$4M – $15M+
Technically its own incorporated town, not a Los Altos neighborhood. Estate country: one-acre minimum lots, no commercial zone at all (like Atherton), no sidewalks, and sweeping bay or hill views. The South Bay's answer to Atherton, with a more rural, equestrian character.
Like Atherton, there are no shops or restaurants in town, and privacy is the product. Buyers comparing the two: Atherton is flat, gated, old-money; Los Altos Hills is hilly, view-driven, and reads more "quietly earned tech wealth." School assignments vary by parcel (LASD, PAUSD edges, and Bullis Charter conversations come up), so verify per address.
One thing to always know
Los Altos pricing runs on two questions: which school district and can you walk to the Village. The city is split between Los Altos schools (LASD feeding MVLA High) and Cupertino schools (CUSD feeding Homestead): the Highlands and parts of South Los Altos west of Fremont Ave are on the Cupertino side, and MVLA-feeding homes generally command more. Los Altos Hills is a separate town entirely. Also useful: the four Los Altos MLS areas are North Los Altos, Country Club, Highlands, and South of El Monte, so searches and comps are organized around exactly these names.
